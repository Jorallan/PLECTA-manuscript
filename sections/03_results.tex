\subsection{Which density axis matters}
\label{sec:res-factorial}

Coverage is the fraction of the frame filled by the rendered bundles, so it
rises both when filaments are added and when the same filaments are drawn
thicker, and only the first changes the grouping problem. A factorial separates
them: twelve geometries, three centreline length densities crossed with four
seeds, were each rendered at bundle widths of 6, 11 and 16 px. Within one geometry the input axis mask and the overlap-aware
centreline reference are byte-identical across the three widths, verified by
SHA-256, while the union of the thick supports changes: areal coverage varies by
up to a factor of \PlectaFactorialMaxCoverageRatio{}. Any movement in the score
across those three renderings would mean that the method or the metric reads the
silhouette.

Across all \PlectaFactorialGeometryCount{} geometries the within-geometry
\Fone{} range was \PlectaFactorialMaxFOneRange{}, that is, exactly zero in
\PlectaFactorialZeroRangeCount{} of \PlectaFactorialGeometryCount{}
(Table~\ref{tab:plecta-factorial}). Areal coverage therefore cannot causally
affect the grouping score; PLECTA reads the axis mask, and the metric scores
centreline fragments, so neither sees the width the bundles were drawn at.
Centreline length density does move it: mean \Fone{} fell from
\PlectaFactorialLowFOne{} to \PlectaFactorialHighFOne{} as centreline coverage
rose from \PlectaFactorialLowLengthDensity{} to
\PlectaFactorialHighLengthDensity{}. The coverage bands of the three length
densities also overlap, an areal coverage near 0.31 occurring at all three, so
coverage does not identify a difficulty level on its own. Density strata
reported by target areal coverage should therefore be read as a proxy for the
length density that accompanies them under this generator, and a mask drawn at a
different thickness, as an upstream segmenter may well produce, does not by
itself move the grouping score.

\subsection{Comparison with prior linkers}
\label{sec:res-comparators}

Three comparators were run on identical input masks with one scorer: a greedy
minimum-turning-angle continuation rule, which tests the paper's central claim
directly, and two published methods, DNAi \citep{playout2026dnai} and GraFT
\citep{osterlund2026graft}, each executed from its own released source. All were
scored through the same conversion and metric code as PLECTA, and that path was
gated on parity: before any comparator number was believed it had to reproduce
PLECTA's stored \Fone{}, adjusted Rand index and both variation-of-information
components, because a faulty conversion yields plausible rather than obviously
wrong scores. Each comparator received a parameter translation selected on the
same \PlectaDevelopmentSceneCount{} development scenes PLECTA was configured on,
so none is disadvantaged by tuning effort, and every adaptation described below
was checked rather than assumed and found to favour the comparator.

\textbf{Greedy continuation.} The contribution claimed in
Section~\ref{sec:intro} is that a junction is better solved as one exact
matching, with an explicit priced option to leave an arm unmatched, than by
pairing endpoints greedily in priority order, the rule used by SIFNE, by GraFT's
path cover, and by the continuity baseline of Wegmayr et al.\
\citep{zhang2017sifne,osterlund2026graft,wegmayr2020microplastic}. The
comparator therefore skeletonises the same mask, prunes spurs, splits the
skeleton into branches, and greedily accepts the lowest-turning-angle pairing
between branch ends within a distance and a turning-angle gate, both gates fixed
by a grid sweep for best mean development \Fone{}. On
\PlectaGreedySceneCount{} scenes generated after both configurations were fixed,
PLECTA scored \PlectaGreedyPlectaFOne{} against
\PlectaGreedySweptBaselineFOne{}, a paired difference of
\PlectaGreedySweptDeltaFOne{} (95\% CI [\PlectaGreedySweptDeltaCILow{},
\PlectaGreedySweptDeltaCIHigh{}]), and was better in
\PlectaGreedySweptWinCount{} of \PlectaGreedySceneCount{} scenes
(Table~\ref{tab:plecta-greedy}). Adjusted Rand index and variation of
information rank the two the same way, so the result does not depend on the
in-house measure. The gap is widest in recall and in reference-instance
recovery, the expected signature: a greedy pass commits to the locally
straightest continuation and cannot revise it when that choice strands a better
partner, so single filaments are left divided. On clean axes the difference was
\PlectaGreedySweptCleanDeltaFOne{}
[\PlectaGreedySweptCleanDeltaCILow{}, \PlectaGreedySweptCleanDeltaCIHigh{}], so
it is not an artefact of mask degradation.

\textbf{DNAi.} DNAi post-processes a binary segmentation by the sequence
described in Section~\ref{sec:intro}; its segmentation network was bypassed and
only that stage used, so grouping is not confounded with segmentation quality.
At its released defaults it scored \Fone{}=\PlectaDNAiShippedFOne{} on the
degraded masks, which is not a fair measure of it: its single-linkage clustering
of junction pixels at 10~px chains together crossings that lie closer than that,
and at 60\% coverage the largest cluster spans 89~px and is replaced by one
small erasure disk, leaving the crossings along it unseparated and the whole
tangle a single instance. Reducing that distance to 2~px on development scenes
raised development \Fone{} from 0.211 to 0.378 and cut variation-of-information
merge from 2.07 to 0.66, confirming the mechanism; dilating the input to a
thicker mask was also tested across 41 development configurations and made DNAi
worse at every setting, so it was not used. At its best development-selected
configuration DNAi scored \PlectaDNAiFOne{} against PLECTA's
\PlectaDNAiPlectaFOne{}, a paired difference of \PlectaDNAiDeltaFOne{} (95\% CI
[\PlectaDNAiDeltaCILow{}, \PlectaDNAiDeltaCIHigh{}]) over
\PlectaDNAiSceneCount{} scenes, and \PlectaDNAiCleanDeltaFOne{}
[\PlectaDNAiCleanDeltaCILow{}, \PlectaDNAiCleanDeltaCIHigh{}] on clean axes,
the gap widening monotonically with density (Figure~\ref{fig:comparators}).
Both methods must be read against the one-instance-per-connected-component floor
at their own density, since that floor is itself strongly density-dependent and
a pooled value would flatter any method at 60\% coverage.

That margin is a limitation of domain and density, not a general ranking. DNAi
is built for DNA-fibre micrographs, which are sparse: its own bundled test image
contains five junction clusters, whereas our scenes contain between 23 and 183,
and where the network is sparse and the axis clean it recovers
\PlectaDNAiCleanSparseFOne{} at 20\% coverage. Two structural differences
explain the rest. It commits to a pairing at every junction of degree two to
four with no cost threshold, so it cannot decline a continuation the way a
priced unmatched option can; and it resolves only junctions of degree at most
four. That cap is easy to underrate by counting junctions, since junctions of
degree five or more are only about a tenth of the clusters here, but weighted by
the evidence the metric actually counts they carry
\PlectaEvidenceHighDegree{}\,\% of same-filament fragment pairs on the degraded
masks and \PlectaEvidenceCleanHighDegree{}\,\% on the clean ones, because a
single such junction severs every pair that spans it.

Restricting the score to the evidence a degree-limited, gap-blind method can
reach separates capability from judgement. On clean axes the difference falls
from \PlectaDNAiCleanDeltaFOne{} to \PlectaDNAiRestrictedCleanDeltaFOne{}
[\PlectaDNAiRestrictedCleanDeltaCILow{},
\PlectaDNAiRestrictedCleanDeltaCIHigh{}], still favouring PLECTA in
\PlectaDNAiRestrictedCleanWinCount{} of \PlectaDNAiCleanSceneCount{} scenes and
at every coverage level. DNAi gains far more from the restriction than PLECTA
does, which is what shows the restriction genuinely removes its handicap. So
roughly \PlectaDNAiCoverageShareClean{}\,\% of the margin is coverage of cases
the comparator cannot attempt and \PlectaDNAiDecisionShareClean{}\,\% is the
outcome of decisions both methods make. Consistent with that, after its
clustering is repaired DNAi's remaining errors are dominated by division rather
than merging, and a third of those divisions occur at junctions of degree four,
which it does attempt: its matcher commits to a pairing there with no rejection
threshold.

\textbf{GraFT.} GraFT builds a graph on a skeleton, derives an angle threshold
per component, and traces filaments by constrained depth-first search followed
by a greedy longest-first path cover. Its published entry point begins with a
Frangi and Otsu front end that enhances and binarises grayscale ridge images,
which has nothing to do on a two-valued axis, so the mask was injected at its
skeleton-to-graph stage and everything downstream is its own code unchanged.
That substitution favours GraFT: run end to end on the grayscale images it
scores \PlectaGraFTNativeFOne{}, near the connectivity floor, because its
segmentation stage recovers only a third to a half of the reference skeleton.
Its minimum continuation angle was re-tuned on development scenes, gaining it a
further 0.04. GraFT scored \PlectaGraFTFOne{}, a paired difference of
\PlectaGraFTDeltaFOne{} [\PlectaGraFTDeltaCILow{}, \PlectaGraFTDeltaCIHigh{}]
favouring PLECTA in every one of the \PlectaGraFTSceneCount{} scenes both
methods completed, and \PlectaGraFTCleanDeltaFOne{}
[\PlectaGraFTCleanDeltaCILow{}, \PlectaGraFTCleanDeltaCIHigh{}] on clean axes.
Its errors are divisions rather than merges (variation of information split
\PlectaGraFTVISplit{} against merge \PlectaGraFTVIMerge{}), which follows from a
greedy cover that cannot revise an early commitment once the scene collapses
into one connected graph, as it does above 30\% coverage.
\PlectaGraFTFailed{} of the densest degraded scenes did not finish inside a
45-minute budget and are excluded from its means; they are the hardest scenes of
the set, so that exclusion flatters GraFT. Neither released method establishes a
general ranking, and Section~\ref{sec:limitations} states what these comparisons
leave untested.

\subsection{Crossing resolution and crossing representation}
\label{sec:res-overlap}

Two distinct things are meant by an overlap-aware result, and the metric treats
them differently. The first is whether a crossing is \emph{resolved} correctly: of the arms
meeting at a junction, which continues into which. This is what the
common-fragment metric measures, and it is the difficult decision. At a
four-arm crossing the junction pixels are removed and the four arms become four
fragments; pairing the correct two arms puts both pairs in agreement with the
reference and counts them as true positives, while pairing the wrong two
produces a false positive and a false negative. Every score in this paper
therefore rewards resolving crossings correctly, and the comparators lose
precisely because they resolve them worse.

The second is whether the emitted raster \emph{represents} a crossing pixel as
belonging to both filaments. That is a property of the output format rather
than of the grouping decision, and the metric does not see it: the junction
pixels it would have to inspect are the ones it discards when building
fragments. Flattening either method's layers to a single label image accordingly
changes \Fone{} by at most \PlectaOverlapFlattenCost{} for PLECTA and 0.002
for GraFT.

The representation is instead verifiable directly: on the
comparison set, reference instances share \PlectaOverlapReferenceLow{}\,\% of
their pixels with another instance at 20\% coverage, rising to
\PlectaOverlapReferenceHigh{}\,\% at 60\%. PLECTA emits
\PlectaOverlapExpressedLow{}--\PlectaOverlapExpressedHigh{}\,\% shared pixels;
GraFT, whose output is close to a hard partition, about
\PlectaOverlapGraFTHigh{}\,\%.

One consequence matters for the comparison above. GraFT's near-hard partition
is a real structural limitation, but it costs GraFT nothing under this metric,
so its deficit is not an artefact of that limitation: it loses on how it
resolves crossings, not on how it draws them. The converse also holds, and is
the honest limit of what is claimed here. PLECTA's shared-pixel output is
reported as a measured property of the representation, not as something the
\Fone{} rewards, and a metric scoring pixel-level crossing ownership directly
would test the two claims separately.

Figure~\ref{fig:examples} shows what the score corresponds to scene by scene,
taking at three coverage levels the scene whose \Fone{} is closest to the
median, so the rows are typical rather than selected. The grouping is scored on
the centrelines in the third column; the fourth adds the fitted widths and is
what the full pipeline delivers, included because that is what a downstream user
sees and because the comparison with the reference is only legible once the
instances are drawn at width. Errors survive rendering unchanged: it redraws the
instances the grouping produced and cannot merge or divide them.

\subsection{Input-mask robustness}
\label{sec:res-mask}

On the separate \PlectaRobustnessSceneCount{}-scene controlled set,
mean \Fone{} was \PlectaRobustnessDegradedFOneMean{} from degraded axes and
\PlectaRobustnessCleanFOneMean{} from the corresponding clean axes. The paired
clean-minus-degraded change was \PlectaCleanMinusDegradedFOneMean{} (95\% CI
[\PlectaCleanMinusDegradedFOneCILow{},
\PlectaCleanMinusDegradedFOneCIHigh{}]). Modelled gaps and raster
irregularities therefore caused a small resolved loss under this generator.
Mask thickness costs more (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}), which matters because
it is the upstream segmenter, not the method, that fixes that thickness. Each
width variant is scored against common fragments rebuilt from its own input
mask, so the question asked is how well the method groups the skeleton it is
handed rather than how many pixels it was handed; the decline with width is a
thicker mask skeletonising into a messier graph, not a change in the grouping
problem.

\subsection{Development ablations and downstream rendering}
\label{sec:res-ablation}

One-at-a-time ablations were evaluated only on the
\PlectaDevelopmentSceneCount{} development scenes
(Table~\ref{tab:plecta-ablation}).
Relative to the evaluated configuration, removing the junction chord-turn term
changed \Fone{} by \PlectaAblationTangentTermOnlyDeltaFOne{}, removing gap
matching by \PlectaAblationNoGapBridgingDeltaFOne{}, and disabling
connector-based junction-cluster consolidation by
\PlectaAblationNoCrossingMergingAtAllDeltaFOne{}. Every other component,
chain-extended frames and the round schedule included, mattered less, the
largest such change being \PlectaAblationNoJoinPxDeltaFOne{}. These are
development values, not held-out effect estimates.

The core output is a grouping, not a set of connected curves: accepted gap
links establish identity but are not painted, so on the development set
\PlectaCoreDisconnected{} of core instances arrive as more than one disjoint
pixel run and \PlectaCoreBranched{} contain a branch point. Any downstream step
that assumes one connected curve per instance must account for this. Default
Stage~4 ribbon rendering scored
development \Fone{}=\PlectaStageFourFOne{}, a change of
\PlectaStageFourDeltaFOne{}, and reduced those fractions to
\PlectaStageFourDisconnected{} and \PlectaStageFourBranched{}, so rendering buys
connectivity at a small cost in the identity score without changing chain
membership. Enabling the separate 0.6 silhouette-absorption rule reduced
\Fone{} further to \PlectaStageFourAbsorbFOne{}, and because absorption can
merge identities it remained disabled. None of these topology measurements
validates apparent width.

The negative control of Section~\ref{sec:res-factorial} is worth seeing scene
by scene (Figure~\ref{fig:performance}), because a mean can hide a null. It is
also worth recording that PLECTA's errors are two-sided: a fragment \Fone{} can
be reached one-sidedly, by merging everything or by dividing everything, and
PLECTA sits on the precision--recall diagonal where the connected-component
floor recovers \PlectaCCFloorRecall{} of same-filament pairs at a precision of
only \PlectaCCFloorPrecision{}, merging every filament of a connected network
into one instance.
