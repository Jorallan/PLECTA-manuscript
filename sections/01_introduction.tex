\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many materials comprise networks of long, overlapping strands: carbon-nanotube
sheets, collagen in connective tissue, paper, nonwoven textiles,
fibre-reinforced composites and twisted ropes
\citep{wang2015twisting,allan2025rope}. Their effective properties, from
stiffness and strength to conductivity and fracture resistance, depend on
network geometry, constituent behaviour and inter-strand interactions. For
example, load transfer between nanotubes can limit the stiffness and strength
of a nanotube assembly despite the high modulus of each tube
\citep{devolder2013cnt,coleman2006small,volkov2010structural,%
ostanin2013distinct,ostanin2019size,wittmaack2018mesoscopic}.
Predictive models therefore combine constitutive and interaction laws with
geometric inputs: each strand's length, thickness, orientation, local curvature
and connectivity. Outside materials science, DNA fibre spreading assays measure
the length ratio between two labelled replication tracts along individual
fibres \citep{playout2026dnai}, while time-resolved actin imaging tracks
filament reorganisation within living cells \citep{osterlund2026graft}.

Micrographs show strand networks, but routine image analysis yields only bulk
measures such as coverage and orientation
\citep{dong2022detecting,imtiaz2023facile,beigzadeh2024utilizing}. Measuring
strand-length distributions or crossings requires each strand to be recovered
as a separate, end-to-end object.

This is an instance-segmentation problem with unusual geometry: the objects are
elongated, open curves whose identities must be inferred from directional
continuity rather than from shape or size. Crossings merge distinct strands
into one mask component, whereas signal gaps split one strand into fragments
indistinguishable from genuine ends. Reconstruction must therefore decide which
arms continue through each junction and which tips reconnect across a gap. This
paper addresses that task from a binary strand-axis mask.

PLECTA's core contribution is exact maximum-weight \emph{partial} matching of
all arms at junctions of any degree. Its explicit unmatched-arm penalty allows
genuine terminations rather than forcing every arm into a continuation. Using
only a binary mask, PLECTA combines this matching with global gap-bridging
gates, iterative refitting of tangent and curvature frames along assembled
chains, and overlapping output, so crossing pixels may belong to multiple
strands. These ingredients are individually established; to our knowledge no
prior method combines them, as Section~\ref{sec:related} shows. The
implementation is deterministic: identical inputs and fixed parameters produce
identical outputs.

PLECTA assumes that strand direction remains reliable across a junction, using
tangent and signed-curvature continuity measured over tens of pixels of
arclength. It therefore targets semiflexible networks such as nanotube bundles,
actin, collagen and synthetic fibres rather than chains whose tangent
correlation is lost at that scale; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this
limit.

For SEM images, an upstream CycleGAN/nnU-Net trained on synthetic rather than
annotated pairs converts the raw micrograph to a binary strand-axis mask
\citep{ruehle2021workflow}; PLECTA begins at that mask. This separation isolates
instance grouping and prevents width rendering from affecting the identity
metrics. We evaluate PLECTA on \PlectaHeldoutSceneCount{} procedurally generated
scenes spanning 20 to 60\,\% areal coverage; paired clean and degraded masks
from a further \PlectaRobustnessSceneCount{} scenes isolate mask degradation on
fixed geometry; and \PlectaRealFieldCount{} annotated CNT-sheet SEM fields
demonstrate the complete workflow.

Depth ordering is a separate task: deciding which strand is uppermost at a
crossing. PLECTA infers it non-destructively from brightness continuity in the
corresponding greyscale image: the upper strand retains its appearance through
the crossing, whereas the lower strand's appearance is occluded. The stage
abstains when the intensity evidence does not exceed local noise. To our
knowledge this is the first depth decomposition of a strand network from a
single micrograph. We report it as a demonstration with measured accuracy, not
as a validated three-dimensional reconstruction.

Across held-out synthetic scenes at every tested density, PLECTA preserved
strand identity more accurately than the compared methods, although performance
declined as centreline density increased and remained sensitive to upstream-mask
quality.
