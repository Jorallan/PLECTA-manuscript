\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many materials are composed of long, thin strands laid over one another, some
tangled at random and some deliberately twisted \citep{wang2015twisting}:
sheets of carbon nanotubes,
collagen in connective tissue, paper, felt, nonwoven textiles,
fibre-reinforced composites, aramid fibre ropes and synthetic fibre mats. The
effective properties of such a material (tensile and flexural stiffness,
electrical and thermal conductivity, fracture, tear and impact resistance) are
governed as much by the architecture of the network as by the constituents
themselves, and often more so: the stiffness and strength of a nanotube
assembly are bounded by load transfer between tubes rather than by the very
high modulus of any one of them
\citep{devolder2013cnt,coleman2006small,volkov2010structural,%
ostanin2013distinct,ostanin2019size,wittmaack2018mesoscopic,allan2025rope}. The governing
descriptors are therefore geometric: the length of each strand, its thickness,
its orientation, its local curvature, and its connectivity to the strands
around it. The same descriptors are the measurement target well outside
materials science. A DNA fibre spreading assay reads the length ratio between
two labelled replication tracts along an individual fibre
\citep{playout2026dnai}, and time-resolved imaging of the actin cytoskeleton
follows how its filaments reorganise inside a living cell
\citep{osterlund2026graft}.

A micrograph shows that network directly. Turning the picture into those
numbers remains the difficulty. Routine image analysis measures the network in
bulk (what fraction of the frame is covered, which way the texture points
\citep{dong2022detecting,imtiaz2023facile,beigzadeh2024utilizing}) and does
not resolve individual strands. To measure a distribution of strand lengths,
or to ask how often one strand crosses another, every strand must first be
recovered as a separate object, addressable from one end to the other.

That is an instance-segmentation problem, and its geometry is unfavourable.
The objects are long, thin and open rather than compact and closed; they
touch, cross and hide one another; and what tells two of them apart is not
shape or size but which way each was heading. The consequence is a pair of
failures of opposite character. Where two strands cross, their pixels join
into a single connected blob, the same blob a genuine merge error would leave,
and a method that trusts connectivity reports one object where there are two.
Where the image fades or the segmentation drops out, one strand arrives in
several disconnected pieces, the same gap a genuine end would leave, and the
same method reports several objects where there is one. Both situations are
repaired by answering one question: at this junction, which arm continues into
which? This paper addresses the step existing tools leave open: given a binary
strand-axis mask, recover the individual strands through the crossings and the
gaps.

The contribution is a specific combination, stated here and situated against
prior work in Section~\ref{sec:related}. From a binary mask alone, PLECTA
(i)~solves each junction, of \emph{any} degree, as one exact maximum-weight
\emph{partial} matching in which leaving an arm unmatched is an explicit
option at a fixed price rather than a parity leftover; (ii)~alternates that
junction matching with globally gated bridging of mask gaps; (iii)~re-fits its
tangent and curvature frames along the chains as they assemble; and
(iv)~emits overlapping strands, so that a crossing pixel belongs to every
strand that claims it. The individual ingredients, among them skeleton
graphs, junction excision, continuation angles and combinatorial optimisation,
are established; to our knowledge no prior method combines them, and
Section~\ref{sec:related} substantiates that claim. Degree-general exact
matching with a priced refusal is what allows a strand to end rather than
have a continuation invented for it.

That claim carries a working condition. The only evidence PLECTA reads at a
crossing is directional, namely the tangent, the turns onto the chord between
two tips, and the continuity of signed curvature, each read over tens of
pixels of arclength, so the method applies where direction survives a crossing
at that scale, as in semiflexible networks such as nanotube bundles, actin,
collagen and synthetic fibres. Where that condition fails, as for a freely
flexible chain whose tangent decorrelates within the junction, the method
fails rather than degrades; Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this
limit.

We name the method PLECTA, after the Latin \emph{plecta}, a braid. Every
parameter is fixed in a published configuration and the implementation draws
on no random number generator, so a given mask yields the same strands on
every run; that is the only sense in which it is called deterministic here.

Reaching real micrographs takes one further component, and the workflow is
deliberately divided in two. An upstream route turns a raw SEM micrograph into
a binary strand-axis mask by a synthetic-data-driven CycleGAN/U-Net whose
training pairs are synthesised rather than annotated \citep{ruehle2021workflow};
PLECTA is everything downstream of that mask. The division is what makes the
grouping measurable on its own, against references whose identities are known
exactly, and it keeps width rendering from moving an identity score. We
evaluate PLECTA on \PlectaHeldoutSceneCount{} procedurally generated scenes
spanning 20 to 60\,\% areal coverage, render a further
\PlectaRobustnessSceneCount{} scenes once from a clean axis and once from a
degraded one so that the cost of degradation is measured on unchanged
geometry, and demonstrate both stages together on \PlectaRealFieldCount{}
annotated CNT-sheet SEM fields.

Beyond the plane, a crossing carries a second question the grouping does not
answer: which strand lies on top. PLECTA answers it non-destructively, from
the greyscale already available. What separates the two strands at a crossing
is the continuity of each one's own brightness: the upper strand keeps the
appearance it has on either side of the crossing, while the lower strand's is
overwritten by the strand above it. The stage abstains where that difference
is no larger than the local image noise. To our knowledge this is the first
depth decomposition of a strand network from a single micrograph; it is
reported as a demonstration with a measured accuracy, not as a validated
three-dimensional reconstruction.

The remainder of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews the neighbouring branches of the literature
and places the present method among them. Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes
the synthetic and SEM data, the reconstruction itself, and the evaluation
protocol. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports accuracy on held-out synthetic
scenes, a comparison with four published methods on identical masks,
ablations, the depth stage, and a proof of concept on real SEM fields.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} states conclusions, scope and limitations.
