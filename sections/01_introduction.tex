\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Many materials are composed of long, thin strands laid over one another, some
tangled at random and some deliberately twisted: sheets of carbon nanotubes,
collagen in connective tissue, paper, felt, nonwoven textiles,
fibre-reinforced composites, aramid fibre ropes and synthetic fibre mats. The
effective properties of such a material (tensile and flexural stiffness,
electrical and thermal conductivity, fracture, tear and impact resistance) are
governed as much by the architecture of the network as by the constituents
themselves, and often more so: the stiffness and strength of a nanotube
assembly are bounded by load transfer between tubes rather than by the very
high modulus of any one of them
\citep{devolder2013cnt,coleman2006small,volkov2010structural,%
ostanin2013distinct,wittmaack2018mesoscopic,allan2025rope}. The governing
descriptors are therefore geometric: the length of each strand, its thickness,
its orientation, its local curvature, and its connectivity to the strands
around it. The same descriptors are the measurement target well outside
materials science. A DNA fibre spreading assay reads the length ratio between
two labelled replication tracts along an individual fibre
\citep{playout2026dnai}, and time-resolved imaging of the actin cytoskeleton
follows how its filaments reorganise inside a living cell
\citep{osterlund2026graft}.

A micrograph shows that network directly. Turning the picture into those
numbers is the hard part. Routine image analysis measures the network in bulk
(what fraction of the frame is covered, which way the texture points
\citep{dong2022detecting,imtiaz2023facile}) and stops there. To measure a
distribution of strand lengths, or to ask how often one strand crosses
another, something more is needed: every strand must be recovered as a
separate object, addressable from one end to the other.

That is an instance-segmentation problem, and it has an awkward geometry. The
objects are long, thin and open rather than compact and closed; they touch,
cross and hide one another; and what tells two of them apart is not shape or
size but which way each was heading. The consequence is a pair of failures that
pull in opposite directions. Where two strands cross, their pixels join into
a single connected blob, the same blob a genuine merge error would leave, and
a method that trusts connectivity reports one object where there are two.
Where the image fades or the segmentation drops out, one strand arrives in
several disconnected pieces, the same gap a genuine end would leave, and
the same method reports several objects where there is one. Both situations
are repaired by answering one question: at this junction, which arm continues
into which?

Existing tools answer part of it. Semantic segmentation is mature, since U-Net and
its self-configuring descendants reliably separate strand foreground from
background \citep{ronneberger2015unet,isensee2021nnunet}, but a foreground
mask carries no identity, so it inherits both failures rather than resolving
them. General-purpose instance methods assume the objects are compact: bounded
blobs, centre-directed flows, star-convex outlines, none of which describes a
long open curve that may overlap another \citep{stringer2021cellpose,%
schmidt2018stardist,kirillov2023sam}. Ridge and vesselness filters sharpen
curvilinear signal but assign nothing across a junction
\citep{steger1998unbiased,frangi1998vesselness,sato1998linefilter}, and the
established fibre-analysis packages return network statistics rather than
resolved objects \citep{stein2008fire,bredfeldt2014ctfire,xu2015soax,%
flormann2021finta,hotaling2015diameterj}. Where the imaging is volumetric the
problem is different in kind, because strands that appear to meet occupy
disjoint volumes: in X-ray tomography of fibrous solids and of twisted ropes,
individual fibres are separated by seeded watershed and morphological
reasoning on the reconstructed volume
\citep{depriester2022fibre,allan2025rope}. A projection has no such recourse,
since two strands that cross genuinely share the same pixels. This paper
addresses the step those leave open: given a binary strand-axis mask, recover
the individual strands through the crossings and the gaps.

Recovering strands through crossings is itself well-trodden ground, and the
design space is thoroughly explored. It divides on the two axes that decide
what a method can do at a dense crossing: what evidence it reads, and how it
commits once it is there. One group optimises over a whole neighbourhood but
does not read a binary mask. Basu et al.\ build a spanning tree over centreline
particles, delete every bifurcation, and recombine the segments with an integer
program priced by spline curvature, re-solving until the segment set stops
changing \citep{basu2015extraction}; the re-solving is the strength, since a
recombination that a later fit contradicts can still be undone. DeFiNe covers a
pre-extracted weighted graph with paths of minimum roughness, optionally
letting several paths share an edge, so that overlap survives into the output
instead of being assigned away \citep{breuer2015define}. Deciding several
candidates together is the property that matters at a busy node, and both have
it. What neither has is an axis-mask contract: one consumes intensity evidence
and the other a graph an earlier stage has already built. Both also recombine
chains of at most three segments, which bounds how much of a shattered
neighbourhood any one round can put back.

A second group reads the mask and commits greedily, one pairing at a time.
SIFNE excises each crossing, estimates tip directions from the excised stubs,
and pairs termini inside an angular gate, recovering dense microtubule networks
from the mask alone \citep{zhang2017sifne}. Wegmayr et al.\ pair skeleton
branches at a junction by lowest mutual angle, and where that rule fails they
learn pixel embeddings on patches centred on the junction, so that evidence
beyond the incident angles can inform the grouping
\citep{wegmayr2020microplastic}; the embedding is the better of their two arms
and it is the one that does not read a mask, since it consumes grayscale
context and is trained on instance-labelled crops, and even with it their
tangled fibres are recovered at about half the precision of their isolated
ones, in a regime where two thirds of fibres cross nothing at all. GraFT traces
a Frangi-filtered skeleton by constrained search and covers it greedily,
longest path first \citep{osterlund2026graft}. Greedy acceptance is cheap and
adequate where crossings are rare, but no such pass can revisit a pairing that
a later arm contradicts. DNAi instead enumerates the pairings at junctions of
degree three or four from endpoint angles \citep{playout2026dnai}, which is
exhaustive inside that range and attempts nothing outside it. Learned
alternatives pair termini with an orientation network, detect fibres with a
region-based detector, or trace them recurrently
\citep{liu2019intersection,frei2020fiber,liu2023picktrace}, at the cost of
instance-labelled training data that a binary-mask contract does not otherwise
require.

A third position combines the two, and it is the closest to the one taken here:
read the mask, and still decide globally. De et al.\ reduce a segmented
skeleton to a digraph whose nodes are branches and whose edges are junction
contacts, then partition it by label propagation solved through the
matrix-forest theorem, so that no junction is settled on its incident angles
alone and a decision at one node constrains its neighbours
\citep{de2016graph}. The propagation needs somewhere to propagate from. Labels
originate at root nodes -- DAPI-stained somata in their neuronal images, the
optic disc in their retinal ones -- and the number of instances recovered is
the number of roots supplied. Neurites and vessel trees have such roots. A
tangle of open-ended strands has none, and its instance count is exactly what
is to be recovered, so the formulation does not transfer without a seeding rule
that would have to be invented here rather than adopted. Global reasoning from
a mask, with no seed and no instance count given in advance, is the position
this paper works from.

Two of these are compared against directly here, so what each was built for
matters, because neither was built for this. DNAi is an end-to-end tool for the
DNA fibre spreading assay described above, and it matches trained human
annotators on that task \citep{playout2026dnai}. GraFT traces the plant actin
cytoskeleton through confocal time series, a temporal dimension outside this
paper's scope \citep{osterlund2026graft}. Both are strong in their domains, and both make
choices those domains license: DNAi commits to a pairing at every junction of
degree two to four and attempts none above that, while GraFT's greedy path
cover cannot revise an early commitment. Such choices cost little where
strands are sparse and well separated, which is the regime each was validated
in, and more where strands are dense and crossings frequent.
Section~\ref{sec:res-comparators} runs both from their own released sources, on
identical masks, through one scorer. It also reimplements the recombination
stage of \citet{basu2015extraction}, whose software was never released, and
reports it as a floor rather than as an externally run result.

Skeleton graphs, junction excision, continuation angles, distance gates,
combinatorial optimisation, iterative refitting and overlapping output are
therefore all prior art, and none is claimed here. What is new is narrower, and
it is a combination: from a binary mask alone, PLECTA solves each junction of
\emph{any} degree as one exact maximum-weight \emph{partial} matching in which
leaving an arm unmatched is an explicit option at a fixed price rather than a
parity leftover, alternates that with globally gated gap matching, re-fits its
tangent and curvature frames along the chains as they assemble, and emits
overlapping strands. Each element exists somewhere above; no method there
combines them.
Degree-general exact matching with a priced refusal is what lets a strand
genuinely end rather than have a continuation invented for it. The closest
single prior method is that of Liu et al., which likewise reads a mask,
reconnects across gaps and permits overlapping output, but pairs termini
greedily and depends on a trained orientation network
\citep{liu2019intersection}.

That claim carries a condition, worth stating as scope rather than leaving to
be inferred. The only evidence PLECTA has at a crossing is directional: the
angle between two outward tangents, the turns onto the chord between them, and
the continuity of signed curvature, each read over tens of pixels of arclength.
The working condition is therefore a property of the image: direction must
persist across the junction at that scale, or the continuation is not decidable
from geometry at all. Bending stiffness is what most directly produces such
persistence, which is why the semiflexible regime is the method's home ground:
nanotube bundles sit there, and so do the actin, collagen and synthetic-fibre
networks the methods above were built for. Stiffness contributes to the
condition rather than uniquely causing it, since tension, adhesion, confinement
or bundling can hold a direction just as straight. Where the condition fails,
as for a freely flexible chain whose tangent decorrelates within the junction,
no continuation is more plausible than another, and no amount of matching
recovers what the image no longer distinguishes.

We introduce PLECTA (Pairwise Linking with Evidence from Chain Tangents and
Assignment; also the Latin \emph{plecta}, a braid). It decomposes a skeleton
into arms, endpoint stubs and merged junction nodes, alternates exact per-node
continuation matching with globally gated gap matching while tangent and
curvature estimates expand from local arms to assembled chains, and returns
projected 2-D strand layers in which crossing pixels may be shared. Every
parameter is fixed in a published configuration and the implementation draws on
no random number generator, so a given mask yields the same strands on every
run; that is the only sense in which it is called deterministic here.

Reaching real micrographs takes one further component, and the workflow is
deliberately divided in two. An upstream route turns a raw SEM micrograph into
a binary strand-axis mask by a synthetic-data-driven CycleGAN/U-Net whose
training pairs are synthesised rather than annotated
\citep{ruehle2021workflow,allan2026cntsem}; PLECTA is everything downstream of
that mask. The division is what makes the grouping measurable on its own,
against references whose identities are known exactly, and it keeps width
rendering from moving an identity score. The closest complete workflow is that
of Peters et al., who join a U-Net-like segmentation trained on over a thousand
annotated SEM micrographs to algorithms that trace fibres through
intersections, and validate against human evaluators on fibre number, length
and width \citep{peters2026fibre}. That work and this one divide the task at
the same joint and differ in what the instance step is asked to prove: their
tracer stands in for human counting and measurement, while PLECTA is isolated
behind a binary-mask contract and scored on identity through crossings against
overlap-aware references in which every pairing is known. We evaluate PLECTA on
\PlectaHeldoutSceneCount{} unseen scenes from the development generator and on
\PlectaRobustnessSceneCount{} paired clean and degraded scenes, and demonstrate
the two stages together on \PlectaRealFieldCount{} annotated CNT-sheet SEM
fields, \PlectaRealHeldOutCount{} of them held out from the upstream segmenter.

Beyond the plane, a crossing carries a second question the grouping does not
answer: which strand passes in front. For nanostructured networks that is
normally obtained destructively, by focused-ion-beam sectioning and tomographic
reconstruction \citep{gabbett2024fibsem}, because a micrograph's intensities
record surface topography rather than height. PLECTA answers it optionally and
non-destructively from the greyscale already in hand. The machinery is
deliberately conventional: monocular depth ordering has long derived pairwise
depth from local occlusion evidence, assembled those relations into a
depth-order graph and corrected the contradictions among them
\citep{palou2013monocular,lee2022instancewise}, and single-view hair modelling
resolves the layering of intersecting strands in much the same spirit
\citep{chai2012singleview}. What differs is the evidence available. Those
methods key on \emph{T}-junctions, where one contour terminates against
another; two crossing strands produce no such junction, since both contours
continue through and the mask is a single blob. The cue used here is instead
the continuity of a strand's own appearance across the crossing, where the
upper strand's brightness runs through uninterrupted while the lower
strand's is overwritten, and the stage abstains where that evidence does not
separate the two by more than the local image noise. To our knowledge this is
the first depth decomposition of a strand network from a single micrograph;
it is reported as a demonstration with a measured accuracy, not as a validated
three-dimensional reconstruction.

\subsection*{Contributions}

Three, of different kinds, and it is worth separating them because they carry
different weight. The first is a \textbf{formulation}: a junction of any degree
is solved as one exact maximum-weight partial matching in which refusing to
continue a strand is a priced option, and in which that same price fixes what
may be considered at all, so the refusal removes a parameter rather than adding
one. The second is a \textbf{cue}: depth at a crossing read from the continuity
of a strand's own appearance, which is available where the terminating
contour that classical occlusion reasoning depends on is not. The third is an
\textbf{evidence standard} under which both are claimed, and it is the part
most easily overlooked. Every comparator is run from its own released source on
identical masks through one scorer, and no comparator number was believed until
that path reproduced PLECTA's stored scores exactly. The ranking is re-tested
on a generator built by a competitor, vendored unmodified, with the expected
outcome recorded before the scenes existed. A reference is admitted only if it
reproduces itself when scored as its own prediction, a check that caught a
defect three others missed. And what any real-image score can mean is bounded
by a measurement of how far two human readers of the same micrograph disagree.
