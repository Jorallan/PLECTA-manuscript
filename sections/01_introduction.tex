\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Carbon-nanotube (CNT) sheets, collagen networks, paper and nonwoven textiles
consist of overlapping strands whose arrangement influences their effective
properties. Their stiffness, strength and conductivity depend on network
geometry, constituent behaviour and inter-strand interactions. Load transfer
between nanotubes, for example, can limit an assembly's stiffness and strength
despite the high modulus of each tube
\citep{devolder2013cnt,coleman2006small,volkov2010structural,%
ostanin2013distinct,ostanin2019size,wittmaack2018mesoscopic}. Predictive models
therefore require
geometric descriptors of individual strands, whose identities must be recovered
from the overlapping network visible in a micrograph.

Recovering those identities is an instance-segmentation problem in which the
objects are long, open curves that overlap one another. Crossings merge
distinct strands into one foreground component, whereas signal gaps split one
strand into fragments whose tips may be indistinguishable from genuine ends.
Segmentation must therefore determine which segments continue through each
junction, which reconnect across a gap and which terminate.

We present a workflow that recovers individual strands and estimates their
depth order from a single greyscale micrograph
(Figure~\ref{fig:graphical-abstract}). First, a segmenter trained on synthetic
image--mask pairs produces a binary mask marking the strand axes, without
requiring manually annotated micrographs for training.

The central segmentation stage, PLECTA, then resolves individual strands from
this mask alone. At each junction, it considers the possible strand
continuations together while allowing genuine strand ends. It also reconnects
fragments across signal gaps. This stage requires no training and is intended
for networks whose strands approximately maintain their direction through
crossings. Finally, a depth-ordering stage uses the segmented strands and
the greyscale image to estimate which strand lies on top at each crossing.
Identity is resolved in the image plane; depth ordering is a separate
downstream stage, and no volumetric reconstruction is performed.

We evaluate PLECTA on synthetic scenes and annotated scanning electron
microscopy (SEM) images of CNT sheets. It achieves higher strand-identity
scores than the compared methods on the evaluated dense-network benchmarks.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related
work, Section~\ref{sec:methods} describes the workflow and evaluation,
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the results, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} discusses the conclusions and limitations.
