Many materials and tissues are built from long, thin strands laid over one
another: carbon-nanotube sheets, collagen networks, paper, nonwoven textiles.
Their effective properties are governed by the architecture of the strand
network, so that architecture must be measured from micrographs, with every
strand recovered as a separate object that remains addressable through every
crossing. This remains unsolved where strands cross densely: where two strands
cross, their pixels merge into one connected region, and a method that trusts
connectivity fuses them into one object, while where the image fades a single
strand arrives in pieces and a cautious method leaves it fragmented. We
present PLECTA, a deterministic method that reconstructs individual strands
from a binary strand-axis mask, a two-valued image marking the pixels on
strand centrelines as produced by any upstream segmentation, and from nothing
else. Its originality lies in how junctions are decided: each junction, of any
degree, is solved as a single exact maximum-weight partial matching in which
leaving an arm unmatched carries an explicit price, so a strand may end rather
than have a continuation invented for it, and crossing pixels belong to every
strand that claims them, so the output is overlapping layers rather than a
partition. On held-out synthetic scenes PLECTA leads three released
alternatives at every density tested, and the ordering is unchanged on an
independently published generator run at its own settings. A secondary stage
reads the paired greyscale micrograph to order the strands in depth at each
crossing, abstaining where the image does not separate the pair from the local
noise. The paper demonstrates the complete route on scanning electron
micrographs of carbon-nanotube sheets, from raw image to stacked strand
instances, with no manually annotated image anywhere in the pipeline.
