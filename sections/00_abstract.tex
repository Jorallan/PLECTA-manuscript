Instance segmentation on a dense network of long, thin strands is difficult
because it can fail in two opposite ways: a loose reconnect algorithm ends up
fusing the wrong strands, and a strict one ends up fragmenting them, so that
each strand is split into multiple pieces. A non-perfect binary mask, such as
one from machine-learning segmentation, makes both failures harder to avoid,
because it poses the same two ambiguities at every junction and every gap: a
true crossing, where separate strands legitimately fuse into one connected
component, cannot be told apart from an erroneous merge, and a true end
cannot be told apart from a break left by a fading segmentation. Resolving
either ambiguity takes the same judgement: which arm continues into which.

We present PLECTA, which makes that judgement from a binary strand-axis mask
and nothing else. It cuts the skeleton into arms and junctions and solves each
junction, of any degree, as one exact maximum-weight \emph{partial} matching in
which leaving an arm unmatched carries an explicit price: a strand may end
rather than have a continuation invented for it. Crossing pixels go to every
strand that claims them, so the output is overlapping layers rather than a hard
partition. The evidence is purely directional, tangent and curvature either
side of the discontinuity, so the method applies wherever direction survives a
crossing: nanotube bundles, actin and collagen, synthetic fibres, but not
freely flexible chains.

On \PlectaHeldoutSceneCount{} synthetic scenes from unseen seeds, PLECTA
reaches mean pairwise \Fone{}\,=\,\PlectaHeldoutFOneMean{}, falling from
\PlectaDensityTwoZeroFOneMean{} to \PlectaDensitySixZeroFOneMean{} as coverage
rises from 20 to 60\,\%. Run from their own sources on identical masks, three
released implementations trail it at every density, and repeating the
comparison over \PlectaRegimeSceneCount{} scenes from an independently
published generator, used unmodified at its own settings, leaves that ordering
unchanged.

A secondary depth stage then reads the greyscale to decide which strand lies on
top at each crossing and stacks the network into layers, an ordering otherwise
obtained by destructive sectioning. It is correct on at least
\PlectaDepthDecidedLow{} of the crossings it commits to, against a chance level
of \PlectaDepthChance{}, because it abstains where the image does not separate
the two by more than the local noise; declining up to
\PlectaDepthAbstainHigh{}\,\% of crossings and counting every one of those as
an error, it remains correct on at least \PlectaDepthAllLow{} of all reference
crossings. The method uses no random number generator and its configuration is
published, so a mask yields the same strands on every run. We demonstrate the
whole pipeline on \PlectaRealFieldCountLower{} carbon-nanotube SEM fields, from
greyscale micrograph to stacked instances, with the upstream binary
segmentation trained on manufactured image and mask pairs so that no annotation
enters the route; the manual annotations on those fields are the reference, not
an input. The ordering survives on real masks, and we state plainly what that
evidence does not support.
